# How the Most AI Pilled Company in the World Works

Legacy or Greenfield, the answer is the same. Software at the speed of compute. Build what's next at blitzy.com. That's b l i t z y.com. Welcome back to the AI Daily Brief. There are a lot of companies that talk about their AI transformation or becoming AI native. And yet, most of them, most of us, I should say, cuz I consider what we do at AIDB and Super Intelligent, and now how we got here is all part of this, are both using AI to do the same stuff we've always done, but a lot faster and better, and also perhaps increasingly figuring out new things that we couldn't do before. But the output in products remain fairly similar. The org structure, although pulled and pushed and twisted in new ways, still would be recognizable to someone from 20 years ago. But what happens when you forget all the old rules of building a startup, take the AI pill, and go completely all in on building with agents? That's what Jacob Sandbury did with his company, Spawn.

[16:03] Over the past 2 years, he painstakingly figured out how to build a radically AI-first gaming startup. After a lot of trial and error, his 10-man team is now shipping an impressive product built on a huge agentic stack. And this week, Jacob shared his playbook in a blog post entitled how I built the most AI built company in the world. Now, before we get into it, a brief explanation of what the product is. Spawn is a massively multiplayer game creation platform. If you think metaverse crossed with lovable crossed with Steam, you're somewhere at least in the right ballpark or playing the right sport. Jacob described it as a massively multiplayer game creation engine where thousands of people can be inside one game changing its rules, assets, and graphics in real time on a huge range of devices without lag. Developer Simon Wilson added, "Spawn is a massively multiplayer gaming environment where players can use prompts to the in-game agent Savvy to modify the game world. I fired it up on my phone for a few minutes and was able to prompt a castle into existence, then a dragon, then ride the dragon to fly around that castle." Now, I could do a whole separate show about the instantiation of generative worlds in these sort of massive multiplayer games, but that's not what this show is about.

[17:13] In fact, for the purposes of this show, it doesn't really matter all that much what the product is. The point is Jacob's playbook. And that will work for any company that's at core shipping code and probably for a whole lot that aren't as well. The underlying idea is to take what agents can do now and push it to the absolute limit. As I mentioned, Spawn is a 10-person startup with a team of agents that they call the fleet. Unlike what most people have been building, Spawn's agent fleet is persistent rather than being spun up and spun down for particular tasks. Each agent has their own name and identity, their own memory, and crucially ownership of a certain function within the company. There's a team of agents in charge of maintaining the game engine, another for bug hunting, and another for finance. The agents even get Sundays off for some downtime. Spawn spent $3.1 million on tokens last month, 30 times what they spent on payroll. That makes them one of Anthropic's biggest customers. The fleet shipped 77,000 code changes last month, an average of 108 an hour. But more importantly, having an always-on fleet of agents has fundamentally changed how the team thinks about shipping software.

[18:20] Jacob was clear that none of this worked until the launch of Fable. That model has 500x the number of changes they could ship, but it also meant completely rebuilding their operations around Fable's capabilities. Jacob wrote, "In general, we've seen that OpenAI's models game their own checks. Google's models don't stay coherent for very long, and xAI's models just aren't smart enough yet, though they don't do anything cartoonishly bad. Anthropic's models stay coherent, make good decisions, and can do the work. In my opinion, the thing that holds them back most is that they won't take enough risk." Now, obviously Spawn's way of working is extremely rare, to the point that earlier this year Jacob thought he was using agents the wrong way because his AI bills were so much larger than even his friends were seeing inside big tech. "Turned out, however," he wrote, "we weren't doing anything wrong, we just think about AI very differently. Most companies run big human teams that use AI, and we run a small human team alongside a big team of agents. To figure out what we were doing differently, I compared notes with a researcher at Anthropic. They talked to a lot of customers, and they said Spawn is the one that works most like Anthropic." So, to make this all work, Spawn took a radical approach to building the fleet.

[19:22] As mentioned before, each agent has real system access and clear responsibilities, similar to a human worker. Every day, the agent fleet spawns thousands of helper agents to carry out the granular work. Meanwhile, the human employees use a modified version of Claude code to work on bigger picture items that need hands-on steering. The fleet is the infrastructure that allows the game world to operate. When one player changes something in the world with their personal agent, the fleet is responsible for creating a stable build that can update across every player's device while keeping the game world in sync. Understating it greatly, in my opinion, Jacob wrote, "This is not a simple problem. And as far as we know, nothing else in the world does even a fraction of it. Functionally, the players are working on a live codebase all tied together by the fleet." Now, it was a long and iterative process to get the fleet to where it is today. Jacob explained that most of the time their problems were solved by removing systems designed to facilitate human work, but which made no sense when applied to agentic systems. The first example they ran across was communication.

[20:15] The agents would spend most of their time telling each other what they were doing. Jacob wrote, "Most of the talking in engineering is people saying what they're about to change. Heads up, I'm changing how we store player data, update your work. One message like that touches lots of other work. If every agent tells every other agent, the messages grow much faster than the work. Double the agents and you roughly quadruple the chatter. That is a huge bottleneck. Jacob noted that agents don't actually need to communicate as much as humans because they're not as specialized as humans. With a human dev team, if your database guy isn't communicating well with your front-end designer, you're going to have a bad time. And the result is collaborating on reviewing a pull request to make sure everyone is clear on the code change. Agents understand the entire system deeply, so the communication doesn't really have a purpose. Earlier in the year, in fact, Spawn killed pull requests and just had the agents work directly in the code base. The result is a system that's completely alien for human developers, but seems to work well for agent-only systems. Changes are made with little concern about breaking things elsewhere in the code.

[21:12] If something breaks due to a change, an agent is spawned to triage the problem and fix it. Once an hour, Spawn requires the fleet to have everything in line, and test suites are run every 15 minutes to find problems quickly. It turns out that this way of pushing code is surprisingly resilient because you're not calling humans at 2:00 a.m. to fix a fatal bug. Instead, the fleet is constantly iterating on the code base and fixing problems as they arise. Jacob said that of the 123,000 changes the fleet made over the past year, only about a thousand were reverted. The issue that you might have spotted is that this way of working is incredibly resource-intensive. Spawn runs on 5,000 CPUs, 17 terabytes of RAM, and 150 GPUs hosted on AWS, with another 60% surge capacity on standby. The next big problem for Spawn was the human in the loop. Writes Jacob, "The agents were waiting for humans to tell them what broke in production. Agents are great at reading the world and inching toward a goal. Many teams still run their agents privately on test machines with copies of the product and expect them to update it and ship it to customers.

[22:09] Then humans relay the faults and bugs back to the agents. That loop is a huge mess and a waste of time. In Spawn, the players' agents automatically detect bugs and file bug reports as they navigate the game world. Those are then fed into the fleet along with feature requests or issues in the logs. A small model receives the notification, triages the bug or decides if a feature should be added, and passes it to the relevant agent within the fleet. The entire product is designed to be operated and observed by agents, so humans never need to intervene. This makes Spawn somewhat like self-healing software. When players run into issues, they are detected and fixed automatically. The system design means Spawn operates completely unlike traditional software products. The team considers it an outage if they don't ship changes every hour. And because agents have limits on their memory and context, the system actually functions better when it moves faster. Moving slower blocking ships is dangerous, writes Jacob. A system that moves steadily is much more stable than one that's frozen and suddenly moves again. Getting to a place where we deploy every hour without disrupting our users took a lot of engineering discipline, keeping old systems online for a while, and weeks of pushing on this as a core goal.

[23:13] He described a typical issue which was detected 8 minutes after it affected a player. The cause was named a minute later, and the fix was live 11 minutes later. After that, the platform was stable for the next 4 hours. The next problem was Jacob himself. By October, he realized that his only interaction with the fleet were matters of taste. Should we add this feature? Should we support a new API surface? This was fine at lower scale, but one morning he woke up to 700 requests. He wrote, "The agents were finding questions faster than I could answer them." Jacob also realized that most of these decisions he'd already made hundreds of times before. These changes are too complicated, these ones are fine, something feels off about this one. Spawn had 2 years of these decision logs. So, they formalized them into a set of principles and trained AI judges to apply them. The judges do one of three things. If they can find a human decision on the record, it applies the same outcome and keeps an audit trail that points to the prior human decision. If the question doesn't make sense, it kicks it back to the agent for revision.

[24:08] And if it's an entirely novel situation, the judge hands the question to a human team member. This system means the human taste still guides the product, but in a much more scalable way. And if you guys ever want to get real weird, ask me why this reminded me of Islamic jurisprudence in the 11th century, and I will go deep. The first AI judges came online on October 6th and made more than 11,000 decisions in the first 8 days. Around 7,300 were decided by the judges based on human precedent, around 2,200 were kicked back to the agent, and around 1,800 were passed to a human. Jacob himself only needed to make the call 27 times during that time frame. This unlocked a massive boost to productivity. Before implementing the judges, the fleet shipped around 1,000 changes per day. After the judges had scaled up, the fleet shipped over 7,000. So, with all of these systems in place, the biggest question was what the human team members should do. Jacob wrote, "It's a very different job now. Last year, and even earlier this year, each of our engineers owned a system and understood it deeply. Today, they're closer to paratroopers.

[25:04] They drop into whatever situation needs them, get familiar fast, and figure out what they can do to unlock the agents." He believes there's two new jobs for humans now the agents are doing the grunt work. The first is taste, but that largely involves playing with the product and figuring out what needs to be changed rather than micromanaging the development. It also means telling Claude no, even if something works. There's also a lot of deleting. Each new model changes what the system can do and makes a lot of the work that came before completely redundant. In June, Jacob looked around at 6 months of work that encompassed 70% of the engine, and perpetually felt like it was just one commit away. He scrapped the entire thing and started over. Eventually, he expects the fleet to take over this duty, but for now, ripping out redundant chunks of the platform is a job for humans. The other big role remaining for humans is managing entropy. Jacob said this comes in the form of, quote, "Asking the models for things they wouldn't have suggested on their own. Ambition is one kind, creative entropy is another." Spawn has found that the models are great at building the next 2% of the platform every week, but they're really bad at recognizing that they've headed in the wrong direction for days at a time, and need to take drastic steps to get back on track.

[26:08] On creative entropy, Jacob wrote, "The models also won't ask for 100x on their own, so we do. Some of the prompts we've given Claude include things like, 'Imagine if worlds could be actually unlimited. How do we make a world 1,000 times the size of GTA load on a phone in 1 second?'" That last prompt was the trigger for the latest build of Spawn, the first one that said that really felt like it delivered on the original vision. And importantly, it never would have happened if the agents were left to their own devices. While Jacob believes that this is the way that his startup needs to operate, he also thinks it's a playbook that will be difficult to replicate. I think big companies will struggle to adopt this, he wrote, because none of it works unless you close the loop, and closing the loop takes risk appetite. You have to be willing to let your agents break production. Humans break production all the time. Changes are the number one cause of outages, and humans make changes. What we've found is that agents cause problems far less often. When one does break something, it usually fixes it very fast.

[26:57] Reverts help, but even when something can't be reverted, the agent fixes it quickly as long as it notices. Whether it notices depends on whether you close the loop. He continued, "The other mistake is shoving AI into human-shaped boxes. These things aren't human-shaped. Our agents do a lot of work nobody can fully verify, like customer support. Sometimes they're a little bit wrong, but not that wrong, and they recover fast. When we take them off support, our customers get upset. They'd much rather have an AI that answers right away, gives them the clearest information it has, and can do something about the problem on the spot, than a human who says, 'I'll go look at this,' and comes back 2 days later with a fix. We're not passing our AIs off as human support. We're using them for what they're actually good at." Still, Jacob believes it is entirely worth it. Each new model has made the team work faster and made the system more coherent. Fable was the first big breakthrough that made it functional, but it still had a lot of problems. Fable 5.1 fixed most of those problems, and the team expects that to keep happening as new models are released.

[27:51] "With the right structure," wrote Jacob, "every new model makes the whole company significantly better, and that's very hard to catch up with." There are two other things that stand out to me at first glance as other reasons that this is hard to copy as well. The first is that this is the type of system that's hard enough to build when you're building it from scratch, from the ground up, with all of this intention built in from the beginning. It seems to me to be fairly inconducive to modifying an existing system to this new way of working. I would imagine that if I was trying to replicate this, I would do it in some entirely new division or entirely new product, rather than trying to reapply this way of working to an old section of the company. The second piece is I wonder how much this relies on the product inherently having an incredibly fast feedback loop built in. The self-healing nature of the system that comes from the actual users of the product with their agents automatically reporting bugs is not the type of thing that you're going to get in many categories of products.

[28:42] Does this sort of truly agent first approach require that sort of feedback loop? And if it does, are there ways to build that even into product experiences that don't have a natural spot for it? Still, I think that there are things that other teams can learn even if we're not going to go as far as Jacob. Five principles and practices that stand out to me include one, giving agents ongoing responsibility. Rather than thinking about them as something to be spun up when the need demands it, figure out how to put them in a perpetual system. Practice two is letting the work carry the context. More agents can create more coordination overhead. A shared and up-to-date record makes it easier to see what has changed, who owns the next step, and what remains unresolved. Third, making the results observable. And this gets a little bit into that feedback loop that I was just discussing. The only way for new agents to get up to speed and start to work in a system that predated them is to have visibility into what has been built and what is currently available, meaning observability becomes incredibly important. The fourth one that I think is highly applicable to lots of companies, turning decisions into precedent.

[29:42] Remember, this is where Jacob woke up with 700 requests and said, "Wait a second, we have 2 years of logs of having already made these decisions." And in this way, decisions end up doing double duty, not only solving the issue at hand, but also building the canon of how issues get solved to make things easier to solve in the future. Lastly and unsurprisingly, human attention really is on direction. And I think even more than that, understanding that ambition and long horizon views do not come natural to agents is a really important insight that can shape a lot of other work.
